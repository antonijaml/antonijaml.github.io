Vintage clothing reseller app

Making it easier to judge an item and create a useful listing.

I completed this project as part of the Google UX Design Professional Certificate. The goal was to design a mobile marketplace for buying and reselling vintage clothing.

01

The problem

Secondhand shopping involves more uncertainty than buying something new. Buyers cannot inspect an item in person, clothing sizes are inconsistent, and descriptions such as “good condition” can mean different things to different people.

Sellers need to provide enough information to make a listing trustworthy, but the process should not feel unnecessarily long.

My design question

How could a resale app make product condition easier to evaluate while helping sellers create complete listings?

02

What I focused on

I focused on two sides of the marketplace: people buying secondhand clothing and people creating listings. These perspectives guided the first version of the design and would need to be tested with users.

Buyer perspective

A buyer needs clear photos, measurements, condition details, seller information, and the complete price before deciding whether to purchase.

Seller perspective

A seller needs a quick listing process that still prompts them to include the details buyers rely on.

Questions for future research

  • Which information makes buyers feel more confident about an item?
  • Where do sellers find listing forms repetitive or confusing?
  • Do users understand the condition labels and buyer-protection information?
03

From task flow to wireframes

I kept the structure small and centered it on two tasks: purchasing an item and publishing a listing.

Buying

Browse → view item → check condition and seller → review total → checkout

Selling

Add photos → describe item → select condition → set price → preview

I sketched the main screens at low fidelity before working on the visual design. This helped me decide what information had to appear early and what could wait until later in the flow.

05

Design choices

Specific condition information

Size and condition appear before descriptive copy. The listing form also prompts sellers to photograph labels and visible flaws.

Trust close to the decision

Seller verification and buyer protection appear on the product screen, while shipping and fees are shown before checkout.

A shorter listing flow

The form asks for the most useful information first and allows sellers to preview the result before publishing.

Accessibility considerations

  • Icons have text labels.
  • Touch targets are large enough to select comfortably.
  • Status is not communicated by color alone.
  • Product images would need descriptive alternative text.
06

Future research and improvements

The next step would be a moderated usability study with approximately five participants. I would ask them to:

  1. Find a jacket that they would consider buying and explain what affects their decision.
  2. Locate the full price and buyer-protection information.
  3. Create a listing for an item with visible signs of wear.

I would record task completion, errors, time on task, confidence, and perceived difficulty. These results would guide the next iteration rather than adding more screens based only on assumptions.

What I learned

Working through the project helped me separate the visual identity of the marketplace from its main usability problems. The design only works if buyers can judge an item confidently and sellers can provide the necessary information without unnecessary effort.
